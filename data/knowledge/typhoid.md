# Typhoid fever

## Overview
Typhoid fever is a life-threatening infection caused by the bacterium Salmonella Typhi. A similar illness, paratyphoid fever, is caused by Salmonella Paratyphi. Typhoid is common where access to safe drinking water and sanitation is limited, and South Asia, including India, carries a high burden.

## Symptoms
Symptoms usually begin 6 to 30 days after exposure. Typical symptoms include:
- prolonged fever that rises gradually over several days
- tiredness and weakness
- headache
- nausea and loss of appetite
- abdominal pain
- constipation or diarrhoea
- in some people, a rash of flat, rose-coloured spots

Typhoid cannot be told apart from dengue, malaria or chikungunya by symptoms alone; laboratory tests are needed.

## Warning signs and complications
Without proper treatment, typhoid can cause serious complications, including bleeding or a hole (perforation) in the intestine. Seek emergency care immediately for:
- severe or worsening abdominal pain
- persistent vomiting
- blood in stool or black, tarry stools
- confusion, extreme drowsiness or delirium

## Transmission
Typhoid spreads through the faecal-oral route: eating food or drinking water contaminated with the stool of an infected person. Food handled by an infected person who has not washed their hands properly is a common source.

Some people recover from typhoid but continue to carry the bacteria and shed it in their stool without feeling ill. These carriers can spread the infection to others. Typhoid does not spread through the air, coughing or sneezing.

## Diagnosis and care
Typhoid is confirmed by a doctor using laboratory tests, most reliably a blood culture (sometimes stool or bone marrow culture). The Widal test is widely used, but on its own it can give misleading results, so a positive Widal test does not by itself confirm typhoid.

Typhoid is treated with antibiotics prescribed by a doctor. Drug-resistant typhoid is increasing, including in South Asia, so the choice of antibiotic should be left to a doctor. The full course must be completed even after the person feels better, because stopping early can cause relapse, carrier status and resistance. Self-medication with antibiotics is unsafe.

## Prevention
- drink safe water: boiled, properly treated or from a sealed bottle
- wash hands with soap and water after using the toilet and before preparing or eating food
- eat food that is thoroughly cooked and served hot
- avoid raw food that cannot be peeled or cooked, and be careful with street food and ice
- peel fruits yourself after washing your hands
- use safe toilets and keep sanitation facilities clean

WHO recommends the typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV) for children from 6 months of age in countries where typhoid is common. Vaccines lower the risk but do not give complete protection, so safe food and water practices remain essential.

## Common misconceptions
- "Once the fever is gone, the antibiotics can be stopped." False. Stopping early can lead to relapse, carrier status and drug resistance.
- "Typhoid spreads through coughing or the air." False. It spreads through contaminated food and water.
- "A positive Widal test means typhoid." Not necessarily. The Widal test alone is unreliable; blood culture is more accurate.
- "Vaccinated people cannot get typhoid." False. Vaccines reduce risk but do not offer full protection.

## When to seek care
See a doctor for a fever lasting more than two or three days, especially with abdominal symptoms. Seek emergency care immediately for any warning sign listed above.
