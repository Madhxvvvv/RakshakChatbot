# Tuberculosis (TB)

## Overview
Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It most often affects the lungs, but can affect other parts of the body such as lymph nodes, bones, the brain and the kidneys. TB is preventable and curable. India has the highest number of TB cases in the world.

About a quarter of the world's population is estimated to have been infected with TB bacteria. Most of these people have TB infection (sometimes called latent TB): they are not ill and cannot spread TB. Only a small proportion go on to develop TB disease.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms of TB disease of the lungs include:
- a cough lasting two weeks or longer
- coughing up blood or blood-stained sputum
- chest pain
- fever, especially in the evening
- night sweats
- unexplained weight loss and loss of appetite
- tiredness and weakness

TB outside the lungs causes symptoms that depend on the part of the body affected, such as swollen glands in the neck.

## Transmission
TB spreads through the air. When a person with TB disease of the lungs coughs, sneezes, speaks or sings, they release tiny droplets containing the bacteria, which others can breathe in. Close, prolonged contact in poorly ventilated spaces carries the highest risk.

TB does not spread through shaking hands, sharing food, drinks or utensils, touching bed linen or toilet seats, or sharing a toothbrush.

People with weakened immunity have a higher risk of developing TB disease, including people living with HIV, people with diabetes, people who are undernourished, and people who use tobacco or drink alcohol heavily.

## Diagnosis and care
TB disease is diagnosed by a doctor using sputum tests (microscopy or rapid molecular tests), chest X-rays and other tests. TB infection can be detected with a skin test or a blood test (IGRA).

TB is curable with a course of antibiotics, usually taken for at least six months for drug-sensitive TB. The medicines must be taken regularly and for the full duration prescribed by the doctor, even after symptoms improve. Stopping treatment early or taking it irregularly can lead to drug-resistant TB, which is much harder and longer to treat.

In India, TB testing and treatment are free at government health facilities under the National TB Elimination Programme, which also provides nutritional support to people on treatment. The national TB helpline (Ni-kshay Sampark) is 1800-11-6666.

## Prevention
- the BCG vaccine, given to infants, protects young children against severe forms of TB, though it gives less protection against lung TB in adults
- early diagnosis and complete treatment of people with TB disease stop further spread
- cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing, and keep living spaces well ventilated
- household contacts of people with TB should be checked, and a doctor may offer preventive treatment
- good nutrition and control of conditions such as diabetes and HIV reduce the risk of TB disease

## Common misconceptions
- "TB spreads through shared utensils, food or touch." False. TB spreads through the air when a person with lung TB coughs or sneezes.
- "Medicines can be stopped once you feel better." False. Stopping early can cause relapse and drug-resistant TB.
- "TB cannot be cured." False. TB is curable when the full course of treatment is completed.
- "TB is hereditary or only affects poor people." False. TB is caused by bacteria and can affect anyone, though some conditions raise the risk.

## When to seek care
See a doctor or visit a government health centre for a cough lasting two weeks or more, especially with fever, weight loss or night sweats. Seek emergency care immediately for coughing up large amounts of blood or severe difficulty breathing.
